Nearly six weeks after reporting an 81% quarterly operating margin, Micron struck an even more bullish tone at the KeyBanc Technology Leadership Forum 2026 on August 10. The company said customers remain eager to secure memory despite "very high" prices, with the tightest supply in data centers, where Micron can often fulfill no more than half of customer demand.

Micron's EVP and chief business officer Sumit Sadana noted that the U.S. memory giant now expects the supply-demand balance to tighten further in 2027, potentially making it even more constrained than 2026 as AI continues to fuel demand for DRAM capacity and memory bandwidth.

Micron said its customers consistently point to DRAM — not power, real estate, data center capacity, or logic wafers — as their number one constraint.

## SCAs Lock In Demand as Price Gains Cool

Micron reiterated that it has signed 16 Strategic Customer Agreements (SCAs), which are expected to account for roughly half of revenue, carry binding take-or-pay terms, and run mostly through 2030. Even as these contracts lock in years of demand at prices well above prior cycle peaks, Micron also flagged that it is starting to moderate its price increases.

Sadana explained that Micron is managing multiple levers in parallel: improvements in portfolio mix, gains in overall shipment volumes, and pricing opportunities — aiming for "a very balanced outcome that is in the best long-term interest of our customers and ourselves."

Most SCA volume carries a price band with a ceiling, set using CQ2 pricing. Future SCAs, however, won't be anchored to that same benchmark — any new agreements will have ceiling prices "consistent with whatever the market price is at the time the SCAs are signed."

Per TrendForce, server DRAM contract prices will rise by 13–18% quarter-over-quarter in 3Q26.

## 'Made in USA' Premium Baked Into SCA Pricing

Micron also pointed to its position as the only company investing in front-end memory fab manufacturing in the U.S. as a key differentiator. Samsung's U.S. investments are focused on logic foundry work, while SK Hynix's Indiana project is limited to back-end manufacturing, assembly, and packaging — leaving Micron alone in front-end fab production on U.S. soil.

Customers recognize this distinction, and Micron believes it can command a pricing premium for its U.S.-made supply. That premium has already been factored into the company's SCAs.
